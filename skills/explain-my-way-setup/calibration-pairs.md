# Calibration pairs

Three short tests. Each one explains the same idea in different ways and checks one setting.

How to show a test, in one plain-text message:

1. The heading, for example "Test 1 of 3".
2. The topic line and the question line, exactly as written under each test.
3. The versions, labelled A, B (and C), each as plain text.
4. The answer line, for example: "Reply A, B, C, or none."

Do not use a pop-up question tool for the tests: it hides the versions. Do not save the tests to a file.

Do not say which setting a test measures, and do not call any version "simple", "plain" or "academic". The reader should pick by what lands, not by a label.

If the reader writes in a language other than English, translate the topic line, the question line and the versions into that language. Keep the meaning and the order of ideas exactly the same.

## Test 1 — Starting point

Topic line: "Topic: Newton's second law — how a push changes the speed of an object."
Question line: "After which version do you understand it best?"

**A**
Push an empty shopping cart, then a full one, with the same push. The empty one speeds up fast. The full one barely moves.
So: the heavier a thing is, the more force you need to speed it up by the same amount.
This is Newton's second law, written as force = mass × acceleration.

**B**
Newton's three laws describe how things move when forces act on them. The second one answers one question: how much does a push change an object's speed?
Example: the same push speeds up an empty shopping cart much more than a full one.
Rule: heavier things need more force for the same change in speed. Formula: force = mass × acceleration.

**C**
Newton's second law: force = mass × acceleration. The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.
Example: the same push speeds up an empty shopping cart more than a full one.

Mapping:
- A → Starting point: example first
- B → Starting point: big picture first
- C → Starting point: rule first
- None landed → keep "example first", and ask the reader in one line what was missing.

## Test 2 — Term handling

Topic line: "Topic: why salt melts ice on winter roads."
Question line: "After which version do you understand it best?"

**A**
There is always a thin layer of water on top of ice. Salt dissolves in it. Salty water needs a colder temperature to freeze than pure water. So at −5 °C, pure water stays frozen, but the salty water stays liquid, and the ice next to it slowly melts into it.

**B**
Salt lowers the melting point of ice through freezing point depression, a colligative property: the size of the effect depends on the number of dissolved particles, not on what they are.

Mapping:
- A → Term handling: define new terms
- B → Term handling: assume terms, define only on request
- If the reader says they already knew these terms, keep "define new terms" as the safe default and add a note.

## Test 3 — Depth

Topic line: "Topic: why savings grow faster and faster over time (compound interest)."
Question line: "Which version would you rather get as a first answer?"

**A**
You put 1,000 into a savings account at 5% a year. After one year you have 1,050. In the second year you earn 5% on 1,050, not on 1,000, so you get 52.50 instead of 50. Each year you also earn interest on your earlier interest, so the growth speeds up.
Want more: how fast money doubles, the formula, or why debt grows the same way?

**B**
You put 1,000 into a savings account at 5% a year. After one year you have 1,050. In the second year you earn 5% on 1,050, not on 1,000, so you get 52.50 instead of 50. Each year you also earn interest on your earlier interest, so the growth speeds up.
A quick rule: divide 72 by the interest rate to see roughly how many years it takes to double. At 5%, that is about 14 years.
The formula: final amount = start × (1 + rate) ^ years.
The same effect works against you with debt: unpaid interest is added to what you owe, and then it earns interest too.

Mapping:
- A → Depth: short first, offer more
- B → Depth: full by default
